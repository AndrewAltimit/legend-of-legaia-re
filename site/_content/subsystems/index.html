Subsystems

How Legend of Legaia's engine actually works under the hood - what happens when you walk into a door, how a battle turn resolves, why the world-map ocean shimmers, how a cutscene plays - and how this project's clean-room Rust port reimplements each piece. Each card is a self-contained page on one subsystem: what it does in the game, where its code lives, and how it connects to the rest. Start with Boot path if you're new to the project, or Engine reimplementation if you're here for the port.

The shape of the engine

Everything on these pages comes from tracing the original PS1 code with a disassembler (Ghidra) and then reimplementing the behaviour in Rust. Names like FUN_8002735c are RAM addresses of functions in that trace - the game shipped without symbols, so the address is the name. When a page cites one, it is pointing at a specific traced routine you can find in the project's function dumps.

Legaia is a fairly typical late-90s PSX RPG engine, but with a twist: most of the gameplay logic doesn't live in the main executable. SCUS_942.54 (the game's main executable on the disc) contains the boot path, low-level I/O, the asset dispatcher, the move-table VM, the motion VM, and the audio / renderer libraries - about half the runtime. The other half lives in RAM overlays - chunks of MIPS code the game streams from the disc into RAM on demand, one at a time, into one shared window. There are fewer of them than the subsystem list suggests: the slot-A family (field/town 0897, battle 0898, menu 0899, cutscene/STR 0970, and the minigames) all load at the same base and are mutually exclusive, while the world map, save, shop, level-up, and options are subsystems inside the field and menu overlays, not separate ones. Two of the runtime VMs (the field/event VM and the effect VM) live in these overlays - which is why "zero callers in the executable" never means "dead code" here.

The cards below group subsystems by what layer of the engine they sit at.

Boot, loading, drawing, sound

The plumbing every other subsystem stands on: getting the archive directory into RAM, turning a scene name into meshes and palettes, putting triangles on screen, and getting music out of the speaker.

Boot path
From disc-spin to NEW GAME: the PROT.DAT directory into RAM, the asset-type dispatcher, the 28-slot game-mode state machine, the title screen, and the five-scene opening it launches.
Asset loader
What "walk through a door" loads: scene-name → PROT entries → LZS → typed handlers. Field, battle, sound and music each have a loader with the same dev / retail split; palettes are stitched from other entries.
Renderer
How a TMD mesh becomes pixels: the GTE geometry pass, the ordering table, VRAM texture pages and palettes. The port keeps retail's baked shading by default and cleans up the rasterisation.
Audio
Why the music sounds the way it does: SEQ sequences through the libsnd sequencer into the 24-voice SPU, sound banks per scene, monster banks per battle, and the port's clean-room SPU model.
Cutscenes + movies
Two kinds of cutscene: the STR movies (MDEC video + XA audio, game modes 26/27) and the scripted in-engine scenes like the opening narration crawl.

Field, towns + the world map

Where you spend most of the game: walking around, talking to people, triggering events, and crossing the overworld.

Field / event script VM
The interpreter behind every NPC line, cutscene beat, door and story flag: 43 opcodes in the field overlay, plus the MENU_CTRL sub-dispatchers that open shops, inns and menus.
Field locomotion
What happens between "push the stick" and "Vahn moves": camera-relative direction, per-frame speed, 2-unit steps, and per-axis collision against the per-scene walkability grid.
Motion VM
How NPCs wander, follow and turn to face you, and how the camera follows the player: the two per-actor motion interpreters in the main executable.
World map
The overworld: the walk controller, the encounter / location-entry entity machine, the continent terrain renderer, the fog, and why the ocean shimmers (a palette-walk table, not a script).
World-overview viewer
How the site's 3D world overview assembles each of the three kingdoms into one browsable scene: terrain layout, the retail distance fog, animated ocean water, camera anchors.
VR mode (WebXR)
Put on a headset and stand in Rim Elm: stereo rendering, head tracking and thumbstick locomotion over the world overview, the field viewer and the live play page - same geometry, only the eyes fork.

Battle

From "Fight" on the command menu to the damage number: the scene loader, the per-action state machine, and the arithmetic underneath.

Battle
The battle scene: loader, actor table, character record layout, the per-frame stat aggregator, range / line-of-sight, weapon trails, status ticker, AP / Spirit gauge, and the live gameplay loop.
Battle action FSM
The 47-state machine between “player picked Attack” and “HP has been deducted” - swing timing, Arts chains, Magic, Summons, Spirit, Run and capture.
Battle formulas
Damage, MP cost, accuracy, escape and the RNG - the exact arithmetic, including how half of one equipment slot folds into attack at swing time.
Arts command gauge
Why one Arts-bar button is wider than the others: the weapon-hand command's AP cost is also its pennant width, authored per character and weapon on the disc - which makes it a randomizer target.
Effect VM
Spell flashes, hit sparks and item-use animations: a per-slot state machine over a 32 master / 128 child pool fed by the effect-script bundle.

Menus, shops + progression

Everything that happens in a window: the pause menu, saving, buying, resting, and levelling up. Almost all of it lives in one overlay.

Field menu (status panel)
The pause menu's Status, Magic, Moves and Skills tabs: the 52-record window table, the panel renderer, and the options screen.
Actor / window-widget VM
The small fixed-width interpreter (13 opcodes) that choreographs the UI's windows - the shop's slide-in entrance is one of its programs.
Inventory
One 256-slot bag reached only through five helpers bounded by an active window - the “split bag” that shrinks to 128 slots for a lone character. The “72-slot” figure is a cheat's display page, not a bound.
Save screen
Save-slot selection and the write flow: the nine-state machine, the live game-state window the block is composed from, and the checksum retail re-sums on load.
Shop
Buy / sell / quantity / confirm. Per-scene stock is inline in the scene's script; prices come from the static item table; selling pays half.
Inn
Rest and restore. There is no inn overlay and no cost table - each inn is an ordinary scripted dialogue and the price is a literal in the scene's script.
Level-up
Post-battle XP distribution, the per-level stat-gain table, and the banner - the XP curve and growth tables read straight out of the executable.

The runtime VMs

Legaia's runtime is driven by a handful of small bytecode interpreters that execute scripts stored in the game's data files, so designers could author behaviour without recompiling the engine. They all talk to one shared actor model, serve different layers, and were almost certainly written by different members of the team. Four are clean bytecode dispatchers; the effect VM is a per-slot state machine - a different shape with the same architectural role.

The clean-room port

The Rust port has its own page - goals, the fidelity / enhancement split, crate layering, the runtime architecture, and the legal posture.

Engine reimplementation
Same legal pattern as ScummVM / OpenRCT2 / OpenMW: bring your own disc. Retail is the measured ground truth; enhancements are toggles that leave it bit-identical when off. Playable in the browser on the play page.
